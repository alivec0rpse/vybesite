Tell us the question before you tell us the sensor

The usual way an IoT project goes wrong is not technical. It is that everything gets instrumented, a dashboard appears with forty tiles on it, and within a month nobody opens it because no single tile was tied to a decision anybody actually makes.

So the first conversation is about what you want to stop guessing at: which room went out of range, what is drawing power overnight, where the trolley went. One question answered properly is worth more than a building full of sensors, and it is usually a much smaller quote than you expect.

In detail

A reading is only useful if somebody trusts it.

Placement decides the number

A temperature sensor mounted next to the door of a cold room will read the corridor every time somebody opens it, and the record will show excursions that never happened to the stock. One buried at the back behind a pallet will read nothing but the pallet. Neither is a sensor fault and no amount of software fixes either. Where the sensor goes is a judgement about what you are protecting, made on site with the doors open and the shelves loaded, and it is the part of the job that earns its money.

Alerts that people still read in month six

An alert that fires twice a day gets muted within a fortnight, and the one that mattered goes with it. So the bands are set against what the stock or the equipment can actually tolerate rather than against a round number, with a delay so a door held open for thirty seconds does not page anybody, and an escalation so an unacknowledged alert at two in the morning reaches a second person. Tuning this after a few weeks of real data is part of the installation, not an extra.

Connectivity is where these projects actually fail

The sensors are rarely the problem. The problem is the cold store at the back of the building where Wi-Fi does not reach, the 4G that works on a phone at head height and not on a gateway in a plant room, and the site where somebody unplugs the router every night. That is what the survey is looking for. Sometimes the answer is LoRaWAN because it walks through walls the Wi-Fi will not; sometimes it is a cable, because a cable is boring and works.

One place to look

Every system added to a business brings a login, and a login nobody uses is a system that is not doing anything. Where you already run Vybe ERP, readings and asset movements can appear there alongside everything else, so checking them is part of a routine somebody already has rather than a separate habit they have to build. Where they have to live in their own dashboard, we would rather put five tiles on it that get read than forty that do not.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

Where does a project like this actually start?

add

With a question rather than a sensor. "Which cold room went out of range last month", "what are we still running at two in the morning", "where did that trolley go". Each of those implies a different sensor in a different place, and answering one properly is worth more than instrumenting the whole building and reading none of it. Tell us the decision you want to make and we will work backwards to the smallest installation that answers it.

Can this use the RFID tags you already make?

add

Yes, and it is the main reason this sits under manufacturing rather than under IT. The tags and cards are made and encoded here, so the tag, the reader and the numbering are specified together instead of being bought from three places and reconciled afterwards. If you already run cards from us for doors or attendance, asset tracking can reuse the same numbering rather than starting a second scheme. The chip side of that is covered on RFID and NFC card manufacturing.

What happens when the internet at the site goes down?

add

The gateway keeps logging locally and backfills when the link returns, so you get a gap in the dashboard rather than a hole in the record. This matters more than it sounds: the sites that most need monitoring are often the ones with the least reliable connectivity, and a system that silently stops recording during an outage is worse than no system, because you only discover it when somebody asks for the very period that is missing.

Does it feed into Vybe ERP?

add

Where you run it, yes. Readings and asset movements can land in Vybe ERP so there is one place to look rather than another login and another password. Where you run something else, we can export to it or simply hand you CSV and an API and let your team do the joining. What we try to avoid is leaving you with a dashboard nobody opens because it lives somewhere separate from the system people already have open all day.

Who owns the data?

add

You do, and you can export all of it at any time in a format that is useful rather than deliberately awkward. We will tell you plainly where it is stored before anything is installed. If your policy requires it to stay on your own hardware, that is a reasonable policy and the gateway can be configured to keep it there, but it changes the quote and it is better raised at the start than discovered at handover.

What happens when a sensor fails?

add

It is treated as an event rather than as silence. A sensor that stops reporting raises the same kind of alert as one reporting a bad number, because the failure mode that actually hurts is a dead sensor sitting unnoticed for six weeks while everybody assumes the cold room is fine. Batteries are reported before they die rather than after. Spares are quoted with the installation so a replacement is a swap rather than a procurement exercise.

What does it cost?

add

Quoted, and the site drives it more than the sensor count does. A single cold room is a small job; six buildings with poor connectivity and an energy meter on every panel is not. The survey is where the real number comes from, because cable runs, mounting and how far the gateway sits from the sensors move the price more than the hardware list does. Tell us the site and the question you want answered at sales@vybe.net.in and a figure comes back.
