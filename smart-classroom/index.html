What it takes

A room that works on Monday morning.

Smart classroom projects fail in a consistent and predictable way. The panels arrive, get mounted, work for a term, and then one room has a dead panel nobody has budget to fix, another has a camera pointing at a wall, and in the third the teacher has gone back to the whiteboard because the login never worked. Almost none of that is a hardware problem. It is what happens when a room is bought as a list of items rather than specified, installed and handed over as a working system.

The panel, and why it is not a projector

An interactive flat panel is the anchor of the room, and for nearly every classroom it is the right call over a projector. A projector needs the lights down, which fights the thing you actually want — students able to read and write while looking at the board. It needs a bulb, which is a recurring cost that arrives as a surprise and usually fails mid-lesson. It drifts out of alignment and dims so gradually that nobody notices until it is genuinely bad. A panel has no consumables, is readable in full daylight, and is touch-interactive from the moment it is switched on. The exception is the very large hall where no panel can be read from the back row, and there we will tell you to use a projector rather than sell you a 98-inch panel that still will not work.

Sizing, which is about the back row and nothing else

Panel size is set by the distance to the furthest student, not by the dimensions of the room and definitely not by the budget line. The working rule is that the back row should sit no further from the panel than roughly six times the panel’s height. For a typical 30-to-40-seat classroom that lands on 75 inches; smaller tutorial rooms take 65; wide or deep halls need 86 or 98. This is why we measure rooms before quoting instead of applying one size across a building — a block of classrooms is rarely uniform, and the room that gets the wrong panel is the room that stops being used.

The online class rig, item by item

Teaching the room and a remote cohort at the same time needs five things, and most rooms that claim to do hybrid are missing at least two. A classroom PC to run the session, so it does not depend on whichever laptop the teacher brought. A camera framed on the teaching area rather than bolted wherever there was a spare bracket. A second chat monitor, which is the component everyone skips and the one teachers notice immediately — without it, reading a remote student’s question means minimising what you are teaching, so in practice remote students stop being answered at all. Lighting, because a camera does not need a bright room, it needs light on the teacher’s face, and a classroom lit from directly above gives you a silhouette. And room audio that picks up students asking questions, not just the person standing at the desk. The panel is the shared surface both audiences are looking at.

Power, the part that is always cut first

This is the least interesting section on this page and the one that decides whether the room is still working in two years. A panel and a PC on an unprotected supply in most of India will eventually meet a surge or an outage mid-period. So the install includes UPS backup sized to carry the panel and PC through a typical cut, surge protection, dedicated power points, and concealed cabling. The alternative is the arrangement you find in a depressing number of smart classrooms: a forty-thousand-rupee panel running off an extension board trailing across a doorway.

Extramarks Smart Class Plus, for schools

Hardware with nothing on it is a very expensive television. As an Extramarks channel partner we license and deploy Smart Class Plus, which is curriculum-mapped animated content for K-12 — the lessons where an animation genuinely explains something a diagram cannot, which is most of physics, chemistry and biology — together with LMS modules for assignments and assessment. Because we are the channel partner rather than a middleman, the content licensing and the panel it runs on are the same purchase order and the same support line, and there is nobody to point at when the two do not talk to each other.

Extramarks Forte, for colleges and universities

Higher education has a different shape of problem. The content need is smaller and the delivery need is larger: course structures, assessments, submissions and results across departments that each do things slightly differently. Extramarks Forte is the product built for that, and we deploy it for universities and colleges department by department rather than as one institution-wide switch, because a rollout that starts everywhere at once tends to finish nowhere. We are a channel partner for Forte on the same terms as Smart Class Plus.

Boards, because the panel is not the whole wall

Almost every teacher wants a writing surface beside the panel. Working through a derivation or a correction is faster on a board than on a touchscreen, and the panel should be showing the material while that happens rather than being cleared for it. We are a partner and dealer for JS Global Industries, who manufacture classroom display and writing boards in Bengaluru, so the board and the panel are specified together and the wall gets laid out once instead of being retrofitted after somebody notices.

Handover, and the reason rooms go unused

The single best predictor of whether a smart classroom is still in daily use a year later is whether the teachers were trained on it properly, by someone patient, on the actual hardware, before they had to use it in front of thirty students. Nobody wants to be visibly incompetent in front of a class, so a teacher who is unsure will quietly avoid the panel and it will sit dark. Installation therefore includes hands-on sessions for the staff who have to teach with it, and after handover there is annual maintenance and support — so a failed panel is a phone call rather than a procurement exercise that takes a term.

Where this joins the rest of the campus

A classroom is not a standalone purchase for an institution that already runs software. Where the campus is on Vybe ERP, the room sits inside the same system for timetables, attendance and academics instead of being a parallel setup with its own logins and its own spreadsheet. And the institutional student accounts we set up as part of workspace and email are exactly what make the online side safe: classes admit your own students automatically, a leaked link does not grant entry, and everyone in the session is identifiable. Bought separately, these are three projects. Bought together, they are one room that works.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

What is included in a smart classroom setup?

add

A complete room rather than a list of parts: the interactive flat panel supplied and mounted, a classroom PC, camera, audio and lighting where the room is used for online or hybrid teaching, UPS backup and surge protection, dedicated power points and concealed cabling, content and LMS licensing, and teacher training at handover. Specifications are set per room — a 40-seat lecture hall and a 20-seat tutorial room do not take the same panel.

Should we buy an interactive flat panel or a projector?

add

A panel, in almost every case. Projectors need a darkened room, a bulb that is a recurring cost and eventually fails mid-lesson, and periodic alignment; the image degrades steadily and nobody notices until it is bad. A flat panel has no consumables, is readable in full daylight, and is touch-interactive out of the box. Projectors still make sense for very large halls where a panel cannot be seen from the back row, and we will say so when that is the situation.

What size panel do we need?

add

It depends on the distance from the panel to the back row, not on the size of the room. As a working rule, the back row should be no further from the panel than about six times its height, which for a typical 30-to-40-seat classroom puts you at 75 inches, with 65 inches suitable for smaller tutorial rooms and 86 or 98 inches for wide or deep halls. We measure the rooms rather than quoting one size across a building.

What do we need to run online or hybrid classes?

add

Five things, and most rooms are missing at least two of them. A classroom PC to run the session, a camera framed on the teaching area, a second chat monitor so the teacher can see remote students’ questions without minimising what they are teaching, lighting so the camera has usable light on the teacher’s face, and audio that picks up the room rather than only the person at the desk. The panel itself is the shared surface both audiences see. We supply and install the whole rig.

Do you supply UPS and handle the electrical work?

Yes. UPS backup sized to keep the panel and PC running through a typical outage, surge protection, dedicated power points and concealed cabling are part of the installation. This is the part most commonly skipped, and it is why so many smart classrooms end up with a panel running off an extension board trailing across a doorway.
